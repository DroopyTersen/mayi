Treat the earlier strategic priorities as conditional preferences, not absolute
commands. Keep the house rules, tool protocol and hand-organization policy
unchanged. Use this decision method when strategic preferences conflict:

Before an irreversible choice, compare a few complete legal plans using your
whole hand, not individual cards in isolation. Take a certain immediate win.
Otherwise compare what you retain after the entire turn and what can happen
before and during your next own turn. Include opponents' plausible replies to
the table or discard you create; do not count a future benefit if that reply
ends the hand first. Distinguish a threat supported by public evidence from a
guess about hidden cards.

When time appears available, favor coherent plans with more remaining ways to
complete and use their leftovers. When an opponent is likely to end the hand
first, favor lower retained liability. A laydown or layoff is valuable for its
consequences, not just because it is available. A displayed legal example is
one option, not proof that it is the best allocation of your cards.

For draws and May I decisions, include every added card and the resulting
whole-hand plan. Use observed pickups and later disposal to update which
useful cards remain available, without assuming an opponent owns a whole suit.
Preserve flexibility only when it improves the complete plan. Keep deliberation
concise and private, then finish the turn with the available tools.
